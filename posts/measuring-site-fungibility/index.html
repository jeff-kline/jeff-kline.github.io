Measuring fungibility

Published September 19, 2026 · Version 1.6

Uniformity across sites is a question in any network. A common ideal is fungible sites: any one can stand in for any other. The sites might be plants, branches, call centers, servers, or teams; the metrics might be productivity, error rate, orders fulfilled, or calls handled. When the sites are meant to be fungible, the operating questions are how far apart they are, where the gaps sit, and whether they are closing. This page connects this basic and universal question to a single measure.

The measure is Earth Mover’s Distance (EMD). It has a long history, starting with Monge in the 18th century, and takes its name from the most efficient way to move a pile of earth from one place to another. Most applications compare a pair, shifting one pile onto another. What is different here is that we compare many distributions at once. To my knowledge, this more general form is not widely known or practiced. It came out of an application at a prior workplace, and I have seen the same problem in many contexts since, past and current. A paper I wrote derives several nice properties of this more general construct. I have long wanted to surface this to a broader audience, so I partnered with AI to see what could be done in this direction. The result is the interactive illustration on this page, built on fictional widget data.

A common way to report this sort of view is shown in the table below. It shows just one metric across four sites, it is sparse, and the cognitive load to understand what is going on is high. Below that is an alternative view that conveys far more information compactly, and the EMD falls out immediately as the average of the spread profile: at each percentile, the highest site minus the lowest. The larger the EMD, the larger the spread between sites, and sites that operate identically, that is, fungible sites, have EMD zero. The uniformity control moves the sites toward that zero; raising all output equally leaves the EMD unchanged.

An advanced topic further down covers a surprising and nice fact about EMD in this setting: it combines cleanly across reporting periods. A short bibliography at the end points to related work.

How the EMD is computed

Start with each site’s daily widget counts, sorted from lowest to highest. Lining the sites up by percentile puts every site’s 10th-percentile day beside the others’ 10th-percentile days, and so on up to the 100th. At each percentile, subtract the lowest site’s count from the highest. Average that gap over all percentiles. The result is the EMD, in widgets per day.

Written out, with Qj(u) the count at percentile u for site j:

Φ=∫01[maxjQj(u)−minjQj(u)]du

The two plots show this calculation. The upper plot lines the sites up by percentile and shades the band between the highest and the lowest. The lower plot lifts that band onto a zero baseline, so its height at each percentile is the gap. The dashed line is the average height, which is the EMD.

The number measures how far apart the site distributions are, in the units of the metric. It is not the average output in the table above. It says nothing about efficiency, quality, capacity, or product mix, and a real comparison would need to account for those and for reporting windows. It does not ask whether high-production days fall on the same dates. Duplicating a site, or adding a site whose profile sits inside the existing band, leaves the score unchanged.

The two controls act on this picture. Increasing all production adds the same number of widgets to every day at every site: the band moves up and the EMD does not change. Uniformity pulls each site’s profile toward the average of the four original profiles: the band narrows, and at 100% the sites have identical distributions and the EMD is zero.

The calculation is a single pass over the sorted values. This page’s arithmetic was checked against a second, independent formula and against known cases: identical sites give zero, a common shift leaves the EMD unchanged, and pooled periods average.

Advanced topic · Comparison over time

Network uniformity and Minkowski additivity

My 2019 paper, Properties of the d-dimensional earth mover’s problem, works out several properties of the EMD. One of them, illustrated below, is a surprise. The question is how the network is doing over time: more uniform, or less? Each period has its own EMD, and so does a longer stretch that spans several periods. It turns out the two fit together, and the rule is simple. The interactive example below illustrates it.

This example follows the same four sites through two periods of 100 days each and pools every Period 1 site with every Period 2 site: 16 pooled records of 200 days, shown in the third plot. Pooling here means combining the days of two records into one longer record, as two quarters are pooled into a half year. Treat those 16 as a network and compute its EMD: it is exactly the average of the Period 1 EMD and the Period 2 EMD. This is Minkowski additivity, for equal-length periods.

Minkowski additivity connects uniformity within each period to the spread across all pooled pairings. Making either period more uniform lowers this pooled spread by its share of the average. It does not require the two periods to have the same production levels.

Why it works: at each production threshold, the largest cumulative counts add, and the smallest counts add. Their ranges therefore add, as does unnormalized EMD. After normalizing the pooled 200-day records, the all-pairings EMD is exactly the average of the two period EMDs. Pooling is Minkowski addition of the site records; it does not mean adding daily output values.

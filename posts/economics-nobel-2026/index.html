Stabilizing the economy through expectations

Published October 20, 2026 · Updated October 20, 2026 · Version 1.1

A central bank can help stabilize the economy through expectations. Even when today’s interest rate can’t go any lower, a promise about what the bank will do later can raise spending now, if people believe it. That is a result of the models behind this year’s Nobel Prize in economics, awarded to Robert E. Hall and Michael Woodford. In those models it works because households and firms plan ahead, so their expectations about the future affect their spending now.

I have no background in economics, and I had no intuition for why that should work. I was curious about the mechanism and wanted a plausible explanation, along with a hands-on feel for it: change the model’s settings and see what happens. This page came from working with AI to interpret the result, and it’s an exercise for me to learn a bit about the theory. The interactive charts are computed from the models, whose math and code are written out below; the original sources are at the end.

Here is the case I started with: a recession is bad enough that the central bank has already cut its rate to zero, the floor in this model. It can’t cut any further, so it promises to keep the rate at zero for a while after the slump ends. Today’s rate is the same either way. Does the slump get smaller?

What this has to do with the Nobel Prize

Robert E. Hall and Michael Woodford won for connecting the decisions of households and firms to central-bank policy. Hall showed how to test whether households respond to news about their income the way a careful planner would. Woodford built models that combine households’ spending with firms’ pricing, and used them to ask what a central bank should do.

The explanation starts with a single household deciding what to spend. This textbook example follows one thread of that work, forward guidance: changing spending today by changing what people expect the bank to do later. The prize covers much more. The Academy’s introduction tells the wider story.

The result depends on people believing what the bank will do later. The Academy’s scientific background notes that the model presumes an independent central bank, or at least one able to commit to a rule, though the analysis itself does not require independence. The older argument for independence comes from Kydland and Prescott’s work on why commitments are hard to keep, which won the 2004 prize.

A raise—or a pay cut?

Suppose that at 35 you learn your pay will rise by $10,000 a year starting at 40. Would you spend more now, or wait for the money? And if the news were a pay cut of the same size, would you cut back now?

A simple way to see the idea behind Hall’s test is a planner who spreads what they expect to earn evenly over the rest of their life. For that planner the answer is the same either way: spending moves as soon as the news arrives, years before the paycheck changes, and a pay cut lowers it by as much as a raise lifts it.

The chart below shows this for someone who, without the change, earns $50,000 a year from 25 to 64 and then $20,000 through 85. Each line is a change from that original plan: red for the planner’s spending, blue for income. Someone who spends each paycheck as it arrives would follow the blue line.

Spending can move before income because the planner has savings. Before a known raise, the planner saves less; before a known pay cut, they save more. Someone without savings or credit may have to wait for the raise before spending more, but cutting back ahead of a loss needs no lender’s permission. Over a lifetime, each household in this example spends exactly what it earns.

What Hall tested

Friedman and Modigliani developed the idea of spreading income over a lifetime in the 1950s. Hall’s 1978 paper gave it a test: under his simplifying assumptions, information people already have should not predict their next change in spending. Only news can change the plan.

Hall and Mishkin’s 1982 study fitted a model to families’ food spending. In their estimate, about 80% of that spending followed lifetime income and the rest tracked current income. That is an estimate from their model and sample, not a fixed split in the population. Living paycheck to paycheck can reflect a lack of savings or credit.

As a separate illustration, suppose 80% of households follow the planner’s rule and 20% spend each paycheck as it arrives. Then an unexpected one-time $10,000 gain at 40 raises average spending that year by about $2,200. That number is a model calculation, not their empirical result.

Steering through a slump

Woodford’s model puts planners like the one above into a whole economy, along with firms that change their prices only now and then. Below are five scenarios for the same slump as the opening experiment, each with a different policy. From scenario 2 on, a dashed line shows another scenario for comparison, and the legend names it. Output is everything the economy produces; zero means its normal level.

Scenario

Scenario 1 of 5

A slump begins

Something makes households cautious, and they spend less. For 18 months, the interest rate that would keep spending at its normal level, called the neutral rate, falls from its usual 1% to −5%. It is a real rate, so it already allows for inflation. Everyone in this example knows when the slump will end.

Suppose the bank leaves its rate at its normal 3%. In these conditions saving pays too well and borrowing costs too much. Spending falls, firms sell less, and at its worst output is 9.5% below normal. Inflation falls below the 2% target.

This is a slump in demand. An energy shortage or another supply problem would need a different example.

Scenario 2 of 5

Cut to zero

A lower rate makes borrowing cheaper and saving less rewarding, so the bank cuts to zero, the floor in this model. Output still falls 4.8%. The cut helps, but it isn’t enough.

Zero is not as cheap as it sounds. What matters for spending is the real interest rate: the quoted rate minus expected inflation. If prices are expected to rise 1% over a year, a loan at 0% costs about −1% in what the repaid dollars buy; if prices are expected to fall 1%, it costs about +1%.

That is why zero isn’t low enough here. The neutral rate is −5%, but with the bank’s rate at zero and expected inflation never above 2%, the real rate never falls below −2%, too high to keep spending at its normal level.

People expect the bank to raise its rate as soon as the slump ends. Scenario 3 changes that expectation.

Scenario 3 of 5

Promise one more year

The bank promises one more year at zero after the slump ends. The dashed line is scenario 2. During the slump the rate is zero in both. The promise works through two expectations.

  1. Higher future income. Low rates after the slump mean more spending then, so more sales and more income. Like the planner in the household example, people who expect to be better off spend more now, using savings or a loan.
  2. Higher future prices. Stronger demand pushes up costs and prices. Firms that keep a price fixed for a while set it with future costs in mind, so prices start rising sooner. With the bank’s rate at zero and prices expected to rise, a loan is repaid in dollars that buy less. That lowers the real cost of borrowing, which also encourages spending now.

New spending becomes someone else’s income, which adds to the effect. The worst quarter is now 0.8% below normal. Output rises above normal before the slump is over and peaks just after it ends. Inflation peaks earlier, during the slump, at 2.5%. The bank keeps its rate at zero through the boom, as promised.

The model solves these effects together. It assumes people believe the promise and act on income they expect later. In scenario 5, people believe the promise only once it takes effect; the limits discuss the evidence on the second.

Scenario 4 of 5

Promise too much

Now promise two and a half years. In this model the slump disappears, but a boom replaces it: output rises up to 8.8% above normal, and inflation peaks at 5.6%.

This promise overshoots. The bank has to hold its rate at zero while inflation runs well above target, which is exactly when it will be tempted to break its word.

Scenario 5 of 5

If people doubt it until it’s kept

Go back to the one-year promise, but suppose people doubt it until it takes effect. During the slump they expect the bank to raise its rate as soon as the slump ends, so they don’t change their plans, and output still falls 4.8%. The dashed line is scenario 3, where people believed the same promise from the start.

When the slump ends, the bank keeps its rate at zero, as promised. Now people see that the promise is real, believe the rest of it, and start spending. The result is a boom after the shortfall is over, without the earlier help. The longer the promise, the bigger the boom.

Here belief is an on/off switch: a promise helps today only if it changes what people expect today. Real doubt comes in degrees, and a firm can believe a promise and still wait; the limits say more.

What the model leaves out

  • The economy model is a cartoon: one kind of household, no banks, no housing, no unemployment. The 2008 crisis began in the financial system, which this model doesn’t have.
  • The economy model gives every household the same ability to spread spending across time. Real households may lack savings or access to credit, so better prospects need not let them spend more today. Models with different kinds of households can give different results: in one by McKay, Nakamura and Steinsson, borrowing limits and uninsurable income risk make promises about the distant future much less powerful.
  • The economy model assumes that people spend more today when they expect higher income later, and that they respond to good and bad news alike. The evidence on both points is mixed. In Hall and Mishkin’s estimate, most food spending followed lifetime income. But in the survey by Fuster, Kaplan and Zafar, people said they would barely spend ahead of a future gain, yet would cut back ahead of a future loss; responses to losses were larger and more common. Short-term credit constraints alone did not explain the difference. These were answers to hypothetical questions about one-time private gains and losses, not observed purchases or interest-rate policy. Evidence on central-bank promises is also mixed. Gürkaynak, Sack and Swanson find that the Federal Reserve’s statements moved long-term interest rates more than its rate changes did. In a survey experiment by Coibion, Georgarakos, Gorodnichenko and Weber, though, information about rates more than a year ahead did not further change households’ forecasts.
  • Promises are too powerful in this model. Del Negro, Giannoni and Patterson showed that in the basic model, announcements about rates far in the future have unreasonably large effects today; this is known as the forward guidance puzzle. I chose a weak response of inflation to output (κ = 0.01) to keep the illustration modest. This is a teaching choice, not an estimate fitted to an economy, and it does not resolve the puzzle. With κ = 0.1, ten times larger, the same one-year promise in scenario 3 sends inflation to 21%.
  • There is no investment in machines and no uncertainty about how long the slump lasts. A firm can believe a promise and still wait: if trade rules or taxes might change, delaying an expensive machine that is hard to resell can be worth it. Bloom, Bond and Van Reenen find that investment responds less to demand when uncertainty is high. In a trade-policy setting, a credible commitment can also remove a risk: Handley and Limão find that less uncertainty about future trade policy helped Portuguese firms enter export markets after Portugal joined the European Community.
The model

This is the three-equation New Keynesian model as written in the Academy’s scientific background, with time measured in quarters. Here y is the output gap in percent, π inflation, i the policy rate and rn the neutral rate, all as departures from normal in percentage points per quarter. The charts show rates per year: four times the quarterly departure, plus the normal level. Et marks what people expect in quarter t about the next quarter. When a promise is believed, that is what happens; when people doubt it, the two differ until it takes effect.

yt=Etyt+1−1σ(it−Etπt+1−rtn)
πt=βEtπt+1+κyt
it=ϕππt+ϕyyt

I use σ = 1, β = 0.99 and κ = 0.01. σ sets how strongly spending responds to the real interest rate (a larger σ means a weaker response), β is how much people discount the next quarter, and κ is how strongly inflation responds to output. The usual textbook β and the 1% normal real rate are separate illustrative choices, not a calibrated pair. The third equation is the bank’s usual rule, the Taylor rule: ϕπ = 1.5 is how much the rate rises for each point of inflation above target, and ϕy = 0.5 for each point of output above normal, both stated on annual rates. During the slump and any promise, the rule is replaced by the chosen rate. The rule never goes below the floor. Once the slump starts, everyone knows how long it will last. I solve the 60-quarter path as one linear system, with the economy back to normal at the end, and recheck which quarters the floor binds until the answer stops changing. A promise people doubt until it takes effect is solved twice: first with people expecting the usual rule after the slump, then again from the end of the slump, when the bank keeps its promise and people believe the rest of it. The output shortfall adds up the quarters below normal, as a share of a normal year’s output; a later boom does not offset it.
